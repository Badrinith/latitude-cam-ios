Classic controls, judged on use

The last set chased interesting mechanisms. This one starts from controls people already know how to work, and asks a different question of each: can you use it one-handed, without looking, while the picture is happening?

WHAT "SIMPLE" MEANS HERE

Reachable. A phone is held low and gripped one-handed. Controls belong in the bottom third, near the thumb — not on a top plate, which is the far corner of the screen.

Nothing to learn. If it needs explaining once, it is already too clever. Every control below is one somebody has used before.

Auto is the resting state. The camera works untouched. A control exists to take one thing off automatic, not to demand a decision.

Nothing hidden. No mode you can be in without seeing it. Wrong-mode surprise is the most expensive error a camera can make.

01

Match Needle

Nikon F · Pentax Spotmatic · Canon FTb

One wheel, one needle. Turn until the needle sits in the notch and the exposure is right. You never read a number or know what changed — the camera picks the pair. This is how a generation learned to shoot.

CONTROLS
1 wheel
TO LEARN
nothing — the target is drawn on screen
COST
Can't ask for a specific shutter speed.
02

Everything on A

Fujifilm X100 · Nikon FA

Every dial has an A at the end of its scale. All on A is full auto. Turn one off A and you have taken just that one — the camera keeps the rest. There are no modes, because the dials are the mode, and you can see it.

CONTROLS
3, all resting on auto
TO LEARN
nothing — no P/A/S/M to decode
COST
Still three dials on the glass.
03

Two Wheels, Down Low

Nikon F4 · every modern SLR

Two wheels in the bottom corners, where the thumbs already sit when you hold a phone to shoot. Nothing sits above the frame at all. The single best ergonomic idea in fifty years of camera design, and the one this app currently ignores.

CONTROLS
2, both in the thumb arc
TO LEARN
which wheel is which — once
COST
Only two things without a second layer.
04

Weather

Sunny 16 · every box camera ever made

No numbers at all. Say what the light is doing and the camera exposes for it. Box cameras carried these four symbols for eighty years because they are the only exposure question a person can answer by looking up.

CONTROLS
1 tap, 4 choices
TO LEARN
nothing — you already know the weather
COST
Too coarse for tricky light.
05

The Edge Meter

In-finder meter scale · Leica M6

One strip down the edge of the frame: brighter at the top, darker at the bottom. Slide your thumb along it. It is the only control, it never covers the picture, and it answers the one thing people actually want to change.

CONTROLS
1 strip, full-height target
TO LEARN
nothing — up is brighter
COST
Exposure only; no character control.
06

The Focus Tab

Leica focus tab · zone focusing

A ridged tab you push along a distance scale with one thumb, showing the band that will be sharp. Set it to 1–3m and shoot all afternoon without focusing again — which is how street photography was done before autofocus, and it was faster.

CONTROLS
1 tab, thumb-sized
TO LEARN
the band means sharp — one glance
COST
Focus only. Needs a partner for light.
07

The Advance Lever

Film advance · Leica M · Nikon FM

A lever stowed flush along the bottom edge. Nothing is on the frame until you hook it with a thumb and swing it out, and the dials come with it. The most satisfying gesture on any camera, doing the job of a menu.

CONTROLS
0 until asked
TO LEARN
one pull — the lever shows its own affordance
COST
A gesture between you and every setting.
08

The Only Dial

Nikon FM2 · Leica M — the whole top plate

An FM2's top plate was a shutter dial and nothing else worth turning. One big dial, numbers engraved around it, A at the end of the travel. Big enough to hit without looking, and the honest answer to "which of these six do I need?"

CONTROLS
1, large
TO LEARN
nothing
COST
Aperture and ISO go fully automatic.
09

Split Prism

Rangefinder patch · split-image screen

Manual focus with no scale and no number: the centre of the frame is split, and you slide until the two halves line up. You watch the subject, not a readout, and you know it is sharp because it looks joined.

CONTROLS
1 slider
TO LEARN
nothing — aligned is obviously aligned
COST
Needs an edge in the centre to work on.
10

The Strip

Rangefinder finder readout

A single slim strip under the frame showing what the camera chose. It is a readout until you tap a value, and then that one value becomes a wheel under your thumb. The A at the left puts everything back.

CONTROLS
0 → 1 on tap
TO LEARN
tap a number to change it
COST
Values are small until touched.
